Units of measure in practice

Why units catch real bugs, and how to declare, convert and combine them, including temperatures and whole-number units.

A speed in miles per hour and a speed in metres per second are both "just numbers" to most languages, and adding them compiles fine. Mixing units like that has crashed real spacecraft. In Drachm you can write the unit into the type, and the compiler refuses the mix. This guide shows how that works day to day. The rules in table form are in Units of measure, and Lesson 10 is the gentle start.

Why units catch bugs

A unit lives in the type, in angle brackets: float64<m> is a number of metres. The compiler checks units while it compiles and then forgets them, so a program with units runs exactly as fast as one with plain numbers.

Here is a mistake that plain numbers hide. The function wants an acceleration, and the call passes a speed, which is a distance divided by a time and not a distance divided by a time squared. With plain float64 this runs and gives a wrong answer. With units it does not compile.

Even a unit that is only another size of the same thing is checked. A unit ft; that you declare yourself with no link to the metre is a different kind of thing, and the compiler will not let you assign feet to metres. You have to say how they relate, which the next section shows how to do.

Turning units on

Units exist only if they are declared. in si; declares the common SI ones for you. Without it, m is unknown, and the error lists how to get it:

Put in si; next to in io; at the top of the file. Each file that uses SI units needs its own in si;, as with every module. A number gets a unit in two places: after a type (float64<m>) and directly after a literal (9.6<s>), with no space. 9.6 < s with spaces is a comparison.

Base, scaled and derived units

There are three kinds of unit, and you can declare all of them yourself.

  • A base unit stands alone: unit m;, unit s;, unit kg;. It measures its own kind of thing.
  • A scaled unit is a size of another: unit km = 1000 m;. It measures the same kind of thing as m, so the two mix.
  • A derived unit is made from others with *, / and ^: unit N = kg * m / s^2;. It is the same unit as the expression it names.

in si; declares these for you. The program below also declares three units of its own: ft as a size of the metre, mi as a size of the foot, and mph as a derived one.

Declare units at the top level. ft is 0.3048 metres, so feet now convert to metres. mi is a size of ft, and mph is miles divided by hours, using the SI hour h.

Dividing a distance by a time gives a speed. You do not have to name the result in a unit if the types already say it, but float64<m/s> documents it.

N is the same unit as kg*m/s^2, so the product of a mass and an acceleration fits a float64<N>.

Derived units multiply through, and you can read the answer in any related unit, here kilojoules.

Scaled units of one kind can be mixed in a sum. The result is in the unit of the left side.

Units you declare join the rest: feet convert to metres, and a speed in mph converts to m/s.

Prefixes are not automatic. km is a unit that is declared with unit km = 1000 m;, and not k applied to m. If you want Mm, declare it.

Changing the unit: ::in and ::to

Two conversions look alike and do different jobs.

  • x::in<km>() changes the unit and keeps the number type.
  • x::to<float32>() changes the number type and keeps the unit.

Mixing them up is an error. Asking ::in for a unit that measures another kind of thing is E0407, and the label says so:

::in<km/h>() turns 10 m/s into 36 km/h and stays a float64.

::to<float32>() makes a smaller number and keeps m/s.

Plain numbers in and out

Units do not wall your code off from ordinary numbers. The rules are short.

Plain number in. Where a unit is declared, a plain value gets that unit. mut float64<m> d = 5; is 5 metres, and so is d = 0;. A plain number passed to a parameter with a unit, or returned from a function with a unit, gets the unit as well. This is on purpose: the declaration says what the number means.

Measure out. A measure assigned to a plain number slot becomes the amount in the base unit. float64 x = 250.0<g> is 0.25, because g is a thousandth of kg, and uint32 m = 3<km> is 3000.

In an expression. * and / accept plain numbers, since they just scale: d * 2.0 is still metres. But +, -, % and comparisons need a unit on the number, or the compiler stops you with E0408 and shows the fix. A bare 0 is the one exception, because zero is zero in any unit. (A temperature point is different, as you will see.)

A plain number goes in with = 5 and d = 0, and d++ adds one metre.

Going the other way gives the base-unit amount: 0.25 for 250 g, and 3000 for 3 km.

d * 2.0 is fine, and d + 5.0<m> carries its unit.

Points and deltas: temperatures

Temperatures in degrees Celsius are not like metres. 20 degrees plus 20 degrees is not "40 degrees warmer" in any useful sense, and zero is a real temperature and not "nothing". So Drachm splits such units in two.

A unit declared with offset, like degC, is a point on a scale. The difference between two points is a delta, written delta degC, which is the size of a kelvin.

  • point - point is a delta
  • point + delta is a point (so is point - delta)
  • point + point is an error, and so is a point times or divided by anything

Two points subtract into a delta degC, and the unit text says so.

A point plus a delta is a point again.

A delta is the same size as a kelvin, so it converts to K, and the number stays 9.5.

A point converts with its offset: 12 degC is 285.15 K.

Whole-number units

Units go on every number type, whole numbers too. A whole number cannot hold a fraction of a unit, so shrinking the unit throws part of the value away. uint32<km> km = metres::in<km>() with 1700 metres is 1, and the 700 m are gone. The compiler warns when it happens without ::in:

When in doubt, keep the numbers in float64 and round only at the end, or choose the smaller unit (m) for the whole-number type.

Results with no unit

Sometimes every unit cancels. A distance divided by a distance is a plain number, with the scales folded in: 4.0<km> / 2.0<m> is 2000.0, not 2. A speed times a time divided by a distance is plain too. The result of such an expression is simply a number, so it goes into a float64 with no unit, as the last lines of the program show.

Also in this program, 1.0 / t with t in seconds is a frequency, which the Hz unit names.

1700 m becomes 1 km as a whole number.

Decimals keep the fraction: 1.7 km.

The ratio is a plain number: 4 km / 2 m is 2000, and v * t / 8 m is 1.

One over a time is a frequency.
